## Answer

For Matco brakes, **reverse bleeding is often the easiest method**: push clean fluid in at the caliper and let the air rise out through the master cylinder reservoir.

1. **Confirm the fluid first.** Matco recommends MIL-H-5606 or compatible fluid; its guidance allows DOT 5 silicone fluid, but **not DOT 5.1, DOT 3, or DOT 4**. Don’t mix fluid types. If the system has had an incompatible fluid in it, get Matco’s instructions on flushing and seal replacement.
2. **Use clean, dedicated equipment**—a small pressure pot or syringe, clear tubing, and the correct fitting for the caliper bleeder. Keep the reservoir open and watch it closely so it doesn’t overflow.
3. **Push fluid slowly from the caliper bleeder upward** until bubble-free fluid reaches the reservoir. Avoid excessive pressure. Gently tapping the caliper and high points in the lines can help dislodge trapped bubbles.
4. **Close the bleeder before removing the hose**, clean up any spilled fluid, and check carefully for leaks. Then check pedal firmness and travel.

Caliper angle and internal passage layout affect how easily air escapes. Builders report different mounting angles, so I wouldn’t cut or reposition the strut based on a general rule; check the instructions for your exact Matco caliper, or ask Matco. If you use a vacuum bleeder, bubbles can sometimes be drawn past the bleeder threads and make it look as though air is still coming through.

If the brakes remain soft after a careful bleed, check for leaks, trapped high points, master-cylinder travel/rigging, and seal condition rather than continuing to pump indefinitely. Have the completed system inspected and verify braking on the ground before flight.

### Answer with numbers

For a Cozy, the best starting points are the **fixed-gear and cooling-flow improvements**—they offer worthwhile reported speed gains without the complexity of retractable gear:

1. **Fit well-designed wheel pants.** They’re listed among the highest-payoff modifications, with reported gains of about **8–12 knots**; one carefully shaped design reportedly added about 5 knots. Shape matters: the pants should guide flow smoothly aft rather than encourage it to separate or curl around the wheels. [9][10][12]

2. **Fair the main gear legs.** Reported gains are about **3–5 knots**. Fairings can streamline the struts and address the drag associated with their thickness and the angle they meet the airflow at in cruise. [5][9]

3. **Improve the cowl and cooling airflow.** The estimates range widely—up to **15 knots of potential**—so the result depends on the airplane and the changes made. Avoid bumps and abrupt contours; a more gradual cowl closeout can delay or prevent flow separation. Tufts or oil-flow visualization can help identify problem areas before reshaping. [8][3][9]

4. **Add properly designed winglet intersection fillets.** Reported gains are roughly **1–4 knots**. A well-designed fillet can reduce interference drag, but an ill-fitting one can increase frontal area and make drag worse. [6][9]

5. **Consider nose-wheel doors and careful sealing.** Their speed benefit is described as small or uncertain, but they may help reduce drag by closing openings; sealing gaps and eliminating protrusions are also useful places to look. [10][8][9]

**Lower priority:** Retractable gear is reported to gain anywhere from 0–20 knots, but it’s a major modification and is specifically described as not recommended in one Cozy-modification summary. A spinner’s reported gain is only 0–1 knot, and the presentation notes potential weight and maintenance drawbacks. [9][10][5]

**Don’t add vortex generators solely to reduce drag.** They can lower landing speed, but the cited installations also reduce top speed—about 1–3 knots in one summary, and about 8 knots in another account. [9][1]

These are reported figures, not guaranteed or additive gains. Prioritize clean, smooth airflow and verify the effect of changes on the individual aircraft. [8][14]

## Answer

For a Cozy, the best drag-reduction changes are generally the ones that clean up the landing gear and cooling airflow. The presentations quoted below report speed gains, but treat them as rough, aircraft-specific results—not guaranteed or additive.

1. **Well-designed wheel pants — often the best payoff.** Reported gains are about **8–12 kt** in one presentation; an individual builder reported about **5 kt**. Shape and fit matter: a smooth, close-fitting pant with clean airflow can outperform the original “football” style.
2. **Main gear-leg fairings — strong, relatively practical improvement.** Reported gains are about **3–5 kt**. Fairings should streamline the legs for the airplane’s cruise attitude and avoid bumps or poor transitions.
3. **Improve the cowl and cooling-air exit.** The cited potential is **0–15 kt**, but this is highly design-dependent. Smooth the cowl, use properly radiused inlets, avoid abrupt closure angles, and reduce unnecessary cooling-air leakage while maintaining safe engine temperatures. This is complex work; verify temperatures and clearances carefully.
4. **Seal leaks and clean up protrusions.** Canopy, access-panel, and cowl gaps can disturb airflow. Check for bumps, blisters, and other avoidable irregularities.
5. **Winglet/intersection fillets and blending.** Properly designed fillets can reduce interference and separated-flow drag; the presentation estimates roughly **1–4 kt**. Poorly shaped fillets can add frontal area and make things worse.
6. **Nose-wheel doors and a tidy nose-gear cover.** These may help by closing openings and reducing leakage, though the source gives no dependable speed figure.

**Be cautious about vortex generators.** They can delay separation and lower stall or landing speed, but the reported installation reduced top speed by roughly **1–3 kt** (another builder reported about 8 kt). They are a handling/low-speed tradeoff, not a straightforward drag-reduction modification.

Electronic ignition or fuel injection may improve **fuel efficiency** through better combustion and leaning, but that is not the same as reducing airframe drag. Retractable gear has uncertain gains and substantial complexity, so it is not an obvious first choice.

Make one change at a time, inspect the airflow with tufts or oil-flow visualization where appropriate, and compare speed at the same weight, power, and atmospheric conditions. Keep all modifications within the aircraft’s plans, operating limits, and applicable safety requirements.
