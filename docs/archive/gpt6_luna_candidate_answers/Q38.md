## Answer

The reports describe failures in **several parts of the nose-gear system**, not just the Wilhelmson actuator. A nose-gear collapse can come from the actuator, its wiring and controls, the pivot attachments, or the Cozy’s NG30 structure. The anecdotes below identify reported failure modes; they do **not** establish how often each occurs.

## More commonly reported problems

- **NG30 side-plate cracking or damage.** The narrow area of the NG30s near the sharp corner has been reported to crack, particularly with a heavy nose. In one reported collapse, the NG30 damage was considered likely to have begun well before the final failure. This is an airframe-structure problem, not necessarily an actuator failure. Any reinforcement or repair should be based on the aircraft’s plans and reviewed by someone qualified to assess Cozy structure.

- **Loose actuator pivot/side-attach bolts or pins.** Reports describe the bolts that attach the mechanism to the side plates loosening, sometimes damaging the holes or mechanism before the problem is noticed. One proposed cause is friction between the steel pivot pin and aluminum plate: small movements can transmit torque that gradually backs out a bolt. Don’t assume thread-locking compound or a split lock washer alone has solved it; check the applicable installation instructions and any current manufacturer guidance for the specific installation.

- **Limit-switch and wiring faults.** Misadjusted or sticking limit switches can let the actuator continue driving into its internal clutch, producing repeated loud clicking. Poor crimps, a switch arm rubbing on the structure, or a damaged switch slot have also been reported. A switch can be in the apparent right position but still fail to reliably stop the motor.

- **Relay or electrical-supply faults.** A relay that sticks or has worn/pitted contacts can prevent normal operation. One reported unit worked after engine start because vibration freed a sticky relay. Low battery voltage can also complicate diagnosis, so check the battery and circuit as well as the actuator.

- **Actuator looseness or wear.** Some drive heads reportedly have a little wobble from play or tolerances; wobble alone does not prove failure. A loose fastener on the drive head has also been reported, so unexplained movement should be inspected rather than dismissed.

## Less common, but potentially serious failures

- **Gearbox tooth damage.** A broken nylon gear tooth has been reported, sometimes after nose-wheel shimmy or a severe load. A slow retraction may be a clue, but the symptom alone cannot confirm a broken gear. A sudden collapse can have other causes.

- **Loss of down-position control or free movement.** A failed or intermittent down-limit circuit may allow the actuator to stop holding the gear down; reports describe the screw/drive then allowing the gear to move. This is a serious possibility if the gear retracts despite appearing down. A green indication by itself does not prove the mechanical system is secure.

- **A collapse caused by a combination of faults or loads.** A landing shock, shimmy, wheel-pant interference, loosened attachment, damaged NG30, or actuator/limit-switch problem may contribute together. A nose that drops suddenly does not, by itself, identify which part failed.

Some installations have also been reported to need electrical “down” power to resist the nose gear folding under load, including during taxi. Follow the instructions applicable to your exact system; don’t use a general online report as a substitute for the manufacturer’s operating guidance.

## If you’re investigating a particular aircraft

Do not fly it again until the cause is found and the repair checked. Secure and support the aircraft safely before testing, and inspect the NG30s, pivot/attach hardware, actuator, switch adjustment and freedom of movement, wiring/crimps, relay, and gearbox. Avoid repeatedly cycling an actuator that is clicking or binding. Because these systems and installations vary, use the current Wilhelmson instructions and consult the manufacturer or a mechanic/engineer familiar with Cozy canards.
