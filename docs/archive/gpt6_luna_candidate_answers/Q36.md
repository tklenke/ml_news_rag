## Answer

For a very slight leak, first make sure it’s actually in the tank: test fittings, caps, valves, and the test hoses themselves. Temperature changes and flexing of the unskinned strake can also move a manometer reading without a leak.

**Common ways builders look for one:**

- **Low-pressure test with a water manometer or altimeter.** Use only the pressure specified by the plans or an experienced Cozy source. Then brush soapy water over seams, corners, fittings, and penetrations and watch for bubbles. Don’t use a high-pressure air source—or blow hard by mouth; composite tanks can be damaged by overpressure.
- **A refrigerant leak detector.** Some builders introduced a small amount of suitable tracer gas and used a *good-quality* halogen leak detector. In one report, a cheap detector failed, while a professional detector found a very slow leak.
- **Helium and a helium detector.** This can find tiny leaks, though it may locate where gas emerges rather than the actual defect.
- **Careful inspection of likely trouble spots.** Builders report leaks at seams, tank penetrations, fittings, and the slits where the top skin is formed. Gas can travel through open foam cells and show up far from the leak, so the visible spot may not be the source.

Builders have repaired small leaks by opening the area and filling the defect with flox or epoxy, sometimes adding a glass patch, then repeating the leak test. Any repair should follow the plans’ materials and procedures and be checked again before the tank is closed up.

**Did anyone proceed with an imperfect tank?** The accounts here include a builder who considered filling the tank because a pressure test seemed satisfactory, and a comment that fuel is “the ultimate test.” But they don’t establish that flying with a known, unresolved leak is an accepted practice. Other builders worked to find and repair leaks before moving on. A known leak should be treated as a defect to resolve—not something to test by putting fuel in the aircraft or flying.
