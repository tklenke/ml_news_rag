## Answer

For a very slight leak in a Cozy’s composite strake tank, builders describe several approaches:

- **Low-pressure air and a water manometer:** Seal the tank and monitor pressure with a clear-tube water manometer. Check the test rig connections first—they’re a common source of apparent leaks. Temperature and atmospheric pressure changes can affect readings; one builder also found that an unskinned strake slowly flexed, causing a pressure drop without a leak.
- **Soap solution:** Under low positive pressure, spray or brush soapy water over seams, fittings, fuel-cap openings, probe penetrations and accessible surfaces, looking for bubbles. Foam slits and less obvious areas may also leak.
- **Tracer gas and a detector:** Builders have used helium with a helium leak detector, or a suitable refrigerant tracer with a professional halogen detector. One builder found leaks with helium that were too small to move the manometer. Detector quality matters: another reported that a cheap refrigerant detector missed a leak that a professional unit found.
- **Mechanic’s stethoscope:** One builder reported it was useful for tracing a leak after soap testing failed.

**Finding where gas emerges is not always the same as finding the tank defect.** Gas can travel through the foam and escape far from the actual leak. Testing before the outer strake skin is installed can make locating and repairing a defect easier.

Use the plans’ test procedure and pressure limit, with a manometer and suitable pressure relief. **Excess pressure can damage or rupture a strake.** A slight vacuum can help establish whether a tank leaks, but it won’t produce soap bubbles and is not a substitute for the specified positive-pressure test. Repairs should follow the plans or qualified composite-aircraft guidance, then be retested.

### Did builders proceed with imperfect tanks?

The accounts don’t establish that a builder knowingly flew a Cozy with a confirmed leaking **strake tank**. One builder, after struggling to pinpoint a leak, said he supposed he needed to put fuel in it—but the excerpt doesn’t say what he ultimately did. Another suggested that if a tank **held pressure**, fuel would be the ultimate test; that is different from accepting a known leak.

There is also an account of a tiny seam leak appearing in an aluminum **header tank** after filling, with the engine running well, but it doesn’t establish that the builder knowingly flew with it leaking.

A confirmed fuel leak should be located and repaired rather than treated as acceptable merely because it is slight.
