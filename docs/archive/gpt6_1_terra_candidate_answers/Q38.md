## Answer

The reports describe failures of the **actuator, its electrical controls, and the structure that supports it**. They are individual owner and builder reports—not a statistical failure database—so they do not establish how often most problems occur.

## Repeatedly reported problems

- **Side-plate mounting bolts loosening.** This is a significant Wilhelmson-specific concern. Friction between a mounting pin and its aluminum side plate can transfer torque to the bolt and gradually loosen it. Owners have reported bolts partially or completely backing out, damaged threads and mounting holes, and broken bolts. A collapse can result. One report describes a field test of replacement pins and locking washers, while a later report says that several attempted fixes, including threadlocker and the modified washers, had not been consistently reliable. **The supplied excerpt does not include a complete final remedy**, so obtain current guidance for the installed version rather than relying on an old fix.  
  *Sources: “Wilhelmson Nose Gear Mounting Pins”; “Wilhelmson Nose Lift Emergency Service Bulletin.”*

- **NG30 structural failure.** The NG30s are the aircraft structure supporting the nose-gear mechanism, not the actuator itself. Wilhelmson described cracking or failure in the narrow area as common, with damage possibly developing long before collapse. He recommended a particular fiberglass reinforcement, but that is a reported recommendation—not a substitute for inspecting the actual installation and obtaining an appropriate repair design.  
  *Source: “Re COZY Re Cozy III in need of help.”*

- **Relay and wiring faults.** A sticky relay in one Wilhelmson harness prevented operation with the engine stopped; starting the engine apparently shook it free. Replacing the relay resolved that case. Loose or poorly crimped microswitch wires have also been reported, including in an aircraft with automatic deployment. Low battery voltage can complicate diagnosis, but in the relay case it was not the identified root cause.  
  *Sources: “nose gear issue”; “COZY Nosegear failure or forget?”*

- **Limit-switch adjustment or interference.** If the down microswitch fails to stop the motor before the actuator reaches its internal clutch, the actuator can click loudly at the end of travel. Another owner found a microswitch roller arm rubbing against the mounting area, preventing free movement. A switch can appear to be in position yet still be faulty or obstructed.  
  *Sources: “Cozy Electric noselift”; “COZY Nosegear failure or forget?”*

## Less common or installation-specific reports

- **Unpowered collapse or repeated cycling.** One owner reported that the gear gradually collapsed without down power and, at the down limit, repeatedly cycled as it moved off the switch and the motor drove it down again. That is a serious fault, but the report does not establish one cause or show that every Wilhelmson unit behaves this way. Selecting “down” is not a reliable substitute for diagnosing and repairing a gear that will not hold position.  
  *Source: “More Wilhelmson nose gear woes.”*

- **Drive-head wobble.** Slight movement between the motor/gear head and shaft housing has been described as sometimes resulting from manufacturing clearances. It can also indicate a problem: another owner found a loose underside bolt. Wobble alone therefore does not prove failure—or prove the unit is safe.  
  *Source: “Wilhelmson nose actuator drive head wobble.”*

- **Air leakage around the actuator opening.** Owners describe drafts through the nose-gear area and various seals or boots. This is generally a sealing issue rather than an actuator breakage; any seal must not obstruct gear movement.  
  *Source: “electric nose gear retract air leaks.”*

Reports also mention **broken nylon gearbox teeth**, including after severe nosewheel shimmy. Some of those accounts concern other or uncertain electric retract designs, so they should not automatically be treated as documented Wilhelmson failure modes. Likewise, a slow collapse versus an abrupt one may suggest different possibilities, but timing alone cannot identify the failed part.

## Practical response

If a Cozy shows bolt loosening, structural cracking, unexpected movement, cycling, or end-of-travel clicking, **do not fly it until the fault is identified and corrected**. Have a qualified person inspect the mounting bolts and holes, NG30 structure, actuator attachment, switches, wiring and relay, and gear travel using the instructions applicable to that installation. Support the aircraft securely for ground testing; do not work under an unsupported nose or rely on an energized actuator to hold it up.

A gear-up landing can also result from **forgetting to extend the gear**, rather than a mechanical failure. Automatic deployment and indication systems help only if their switches, wiring, and operation are verified.
