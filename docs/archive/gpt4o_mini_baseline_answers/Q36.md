## Answer

To find very slight leaks in fuel tanks during the construction of an amateur-built Cozy aircraft, builders have utilized various methods. Here are some common techniques:

1. **Pressure Testing:** Builders often pressurize the tanks and monitor the pressure drop. This can be done using a water manometer or altimeter to measure air pressure changes. A typical setup involves sealing the tank and using an external instrument to monitor the pressure. If the pressure drops, it indicates a leak.

2. **Soap Solution:** Spraying a soap and water mixture on the seams, joints, and any potential leak areas can help identify leaks. Air escaping through small openings creates bubbles in the soap solution.

3. **Duster or Refrigerant Gas:** Some builders used a can of "duster" spray (typically containing tetrafluoroethane) to introduce a small amount of pressure and sniff for leaks using a professional refrigerant leak detector.

4. **Vacuum Testing:** Applying a slight vacuum to the tank can also reveal leaks. This involves sealing the tank and sucking air out, then checking for any loss of vacuum with appropriate visual indicators, such as pressure gauges.

5. **Helium Leak Detection:** A highly sensitive method involves filling the tank with helium and using a sniffer to detect helium escaping from small leaks. This method can detect very tiny leaks that may not show up with other methods.

6. **Ammonia and Phenolphthalein Test:** One innovative approach involves placing an ammonia-soaked rag inside the tank and sealing it. If there is a leak, the ammonia will escape and react with a phenolphthalein solution applied to the exterior, changing color to indicate the presence of a leak.

Despite these methods, some builders have opted to proceed with tanks that are not perfectly sealed. There are cases where builders, after experiencing minor leaks, have made repairs, such as applying epoxy to suspected areas. However, the advice generally leans towards taking the time to ensure that the tanks are leak-free before they are put into service. Given the critical nature of fuel tanks in terms of aircraft safety, thorough testing and correction of any leaks before final assembly is crucial.

In some discussions, builders have shared experiences where they found leaks in unexpected places, often along seams or penetrations where additional testing or repair was required. The consensus among experienced builders is that while leaks can sometimes be overlooked, diligent testing and attention to construction methods can significantly reduce the chances of lingering issues.
